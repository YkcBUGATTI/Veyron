1997 → 2005 · From Sketch to Series

An Idea on an Envelope

In 1997, Ferdinand K. Piëch sketched an 18-cylinder engine on a train between Tokyo and Nagoya; in 1998, Volkswagen acquired the Bugatti brand. Four concept cars and countless engineering limits later — in 2005, the idea became reality.

The OriginsOrigins

From a sketch of an engine, to four concept cars, to the first firing of 1,000+1 PS in 2001 — the Veyron's path began with a refusal to accept the impossible.

The Vision

1997: An 18-Cylinder on an Envelope

Ferdinand K. Piëch, then Chairman of Volkswagen AG, set the goal: an output beyond anything in existence. The discussion ran from V6 and V8 to V12 and on to 18 cylinders — a few months later, Volkswagen acquired the Bugatti brand, and the engine found its home.

Back then, there was no literature or empirical data for production engines with more than twelve cylinders — or for production vehicles that could go faster than 350 km/h.

The Name

Pierre Veyron: 1939 Le Mans Winner

Pierre Veyron (1903–1970) was a Bugatti factory driver — in 1939, he and Jean-Pierre Wimille won the Le Mans 24 Hours in the Type 57G Tank. Naming this car after him is Bugatti's most solemn tribute to its heritage of speed.

The 1,001 PS W16The Heart

8.0 litres, 16 cylinders, four turbochargers — more than 3,500 parts, all assembled by hand. At its first firing in 2001, it delivered 1,000+1 PS.

W16 · THE 1,001 PS HEART

Birth of the 1,001 PS Heart

At its first firing in 2001 it delivered 1,000+1 PS; an asymmetric firing order with 45-degree gaps gives it a sound like no other; Ion Current Sensing monitors each cylinder — every detail is designed for reliability and extremes.

The Brief · Four Goals

From Monocoque to W16

The specification gave the developers just four goals: transfer more than 1,000 PS to the road, drive faster than 400 km/h, accelerate from 0–100 km/h in under 3 seconds — and, the biggest challenge, in this configuration still be suitable for driving "to the opera" in comfort and style. A carbon-fibre monocoque of about 110 kg, a front structure of only 34 kg — eight years to turn four goals into one car.

W16 · 01

16 Cylinders in a W

Two eight-cylinder banks in a W arrangement — compact enough to be no bigger than a V12. 8.0 litres, four turbochargers.

W16 · 02

Engine Meets Gearbox

W16 · 05

W16 · 06

The First Drive · 2001

Loris Bicocchi and the First Drive

In 2001, a phone call changed Loris Bicocchi's career — the first drive took place at Michelin's Ladoux test track, in a red-and-black prototype. At the time, the Veyron delivered twice the power of any other production car; there was no reference point, and everything had to be rebuilt from scratch.

I was so excited that I couldn't even wait for the official tests on Monday morning — I went on Sunday.

The RecordsRecords

On 19 April 2005, a production car broke the 400 km/h barrier for the first time; over the following decade, the record was rewritten again and again — by the Veyron itself.

19.04.2005 · EHRA-LESSIEN · GERMANY

407 KM/H: First to Break 400

Test driver Uwe Novacki took the Veyron 16.4 down the high-speed straight at Ehra-Lessien and reached 407 km/h — the first time a production car had broken the 400 km/h barrier.

Veyron 16.4 Super Sport · 2010

The Ultimate Evolution: 1,200 PS

Larger turbochargers, new intercoolers, NACA roof intakes and a new fibre structure for the carbon monocoque — on 4 July 2010, Pierre-Henri Raphanel averaged 431.072 km/h at Ehra-Lessien: the Guinness-certified world record for a production car.

Grand Sport Vitesse · 2012

The Fastest Roadster

1,200 PS and 1,500 Nm, a reinforced drivetrain and race-derived dampers; in April 2013, Anthony Liu drove the black-and-orange record car to 408.84 km/h — the world record for an open-top production car, unbeaten to this day.

BespokeSpecial Editions

From the bare carbon of the Pur Sang to the porcelain of the L'Or Blanc — with the Veyron, bespoke is the norm: 450 cars, no two alike.

Pur Sang · 2007

The Purity of Bare Carbon

All paint removed, the weave of visible carbon fibre laid bare — five cars, the Veyron's answer to "material is design." Anscheidt: "Looking back on the Pur Sang is like remembering the birth of your first child."

The Sang Noir (2008) paid tribute to the Type 57S Atlantic in all-black with chrome details;
the Grand Sport Venet (2012), created with French artist Bernar Venet — art, on the road.

L'Or Blanc · 2011

The World's First Porcelain Car

Created with the Königliche Porzellan-Manufaktur (KPM) Berlin: the blue-and-white wave livery was taped out in Japanese tissue paper and hand-painted over three weeks; porcelain adorns the centre wheel badges, fuel and oil caps, the EB badge and the centre console — one of one.

Super Sport World Record Car · 2010

The Record Car's Black-and-Orange Suit

The livery of the 431.072 km/h record car: black visible carbon with Arancia orange. The WRC edition is limited to eight cars at €1.99 million — the record itself, as a collector's piece.

LegacyLegacy · F.K.P. Hommage

In 2026, twenty years of the Veyron — a new one-of-one honours its creator, Ferdinand K. Piëch.

F.K.P. HOMMAGE · 2026 · RÉTROMOBILE PARIS

An Homage to Piëch

The second creation of Programme Solitaire: 1,600 PS quad-turbo, keeping the Veyron's leaning-back posture and dropping belt line — twenty years on, the original idea is reborn in a new form.

Legacy · 2026

An Homage, Twenty Years On

Unveiled at Rétromobile Paris in January 2026 alongside the original Veyron 16.4. The three-dimensional horseshoe grille is machined from a solid block of aluminium; the red exterior finish uses advanced layering — a silver aluminium-based coat beneath a red-tinted clear coat, creating extraordinary depth as light moves across it. A circular, Bauhaus steering wheel and an Audemars Piguet timepiece set into the dashboard — every detail honours Piëch's 1997 idea.
